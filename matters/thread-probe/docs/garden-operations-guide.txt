Riverside Community Garden Operations Guide

About This Guide

This guide collects the working rules of Riverside Community Garden in one place so that a new member, a new row captain, or a new board member can find an answer without asking around. It covers the old lot only, the one behind the community center on Harlow Street, because that is the lot we have gardened long enough to have rules for. Nothing in here is meant to be permanent. When a rule stops working, bring it to a work day or to the office, and we will change it. The Program Coordinator keeps the master copy, and the version on the gate board is the one that counts if two copies disagree.

The garden runs on three ideas. First, the lot belongs to the members together, not to whoever got there first. Second, water and soil are shared, so what one plot does to either affects the plots beside it. Third, volunteer time is the most valuable thing we have, and every rule below is meant to spend it well. If a rule seems to waste someone's Saturday, it is probably wrong, and we would like to hear about it.

Soil Testing

We test the soil on the old lot every third season, and we test any new ground before the first bed goes in. The soil testing service the county runs charges $36 per sample, and the results come back in about three weeks. A sample is a mix of small scoops from at least six spots across the area being tested, taken from about a hand's depth, dried on a tray in the office, and bagged with the plot numbers written on the bag. Do not take a sample right after adding compost or lime, because the reading will tell you about the compost, not the ground.

The office keeps a soil log in the top drawer of the file cabinet. Each result goes in the log with the date, the plots covered, and any action taken. The readings we care about most are pH, organic matter, and lead. Our ground runs a little on the acid side, which most vegetables tolerate, and the lime we add every other fall keeps it in the mid-sixes. Organic matter has climbed steadily since we started composting on site, and we now add compost to hold it rather than to raise it.

Lead is the test we do not skip. Any plot within 50 feet of a painted structure, an old shed, or the alley gets a lead test before it is assigned, and again if the structure is scraped or torn down. A plot that tests high is not gardened in the ground. It gets a raised bed with a weed cloth liner and fill brought in from outside, and the log notes why. Members can ask to see the result for their own plot at any time, and new members are told about the lead history of their plot before they sign the plot agreement.

If a member wants a test outside the regular cycle, they pay the sample fee themselves and the office handles the mailing. The office will not send a sample without the plot number on it, because an unmarked result is useless to everyone.

Water

The old lot is on a single city meter at the street, and the water bill is paid out of the general fund. In a typical year the bill comes to about $1,150, most of it between June and September. The main line was put in during the garden's second year for $5,400 and has needed only two repairs since, both at the same elbow near the gate. That elbow is marked with a red flag, and anyone digging within a shovel length of it needs to check with the office first.

Each row has a spigot at the path end. Spigots are shared, so hoses do not stay attached between uses, and a hose left on a spigot overnight will be coiled and put in the shed by whoever finds it. Members who want drip lines may run them from the row spigot with a timer, provided the timer is the kind that shuts off on its own and the line is unhooked when the plot is not being watered. A drip run of 45 minutes, twice a week, is the starting point for a full plot in midsummer; adjust from there by putting a finger in the soil, not by watching the plants wilt.

A full plot in July uses roughly 90 gallons a week if it is watered by hand and mulched, and more than that if it is not mulched. Mulch is the cheapest water we have. Bark mulch from the city yard is free for the hauling, and straw is on hand in the shed most of the season. Bare soil in July is a sign that a plot needs help, and the row captain should ask before the plants show it.

Water is shut off at the meter for the winter after the last work day in November and turned on again at the first work day in April. Between those dates, the hose bibs are opened and the lines drained so a hard freeze does not crack them. If you find water running on the lot when no one is watering, close the nearest spigot, then call the office. A running spigot on a weekend can cost more than a plot fee before anyone notices.

Tool Care

Shared tools live in the shed on the old lot, and the count sheet on the inside of the shed door lists 63 hand tools and nine wheelbarrows. Every tool on the sheet has its handle painted garden green so it does not wander home in the back of someone's truck by mistake. Tools that are not on the sheet belong to someone; ask before you use them, and never sharpen them.

Tools go back clean. Scrape the blade, rinse the mud at the shed spigot, and dry it with the rag by the door before hanging it. A tool hung wet rusts by the next work day. Once a month, and always before the winter shutdown, the tool captain wipes every blade with an oily rag and rubs linseed oil into the wooden handles. A splintered handle is smoothed with emery cloth and oiled; a cracked handle is replaced, not taped.

Hoes, spades, and edgers get sharpened twice a season, once at the April work day and once in midsummer. Use the file clamped to the bench in the shed, keep the bevel shallow, and file away from your body. Pruners are sharpened by the tool captain only, because a pruner sharpened at the wrong angle is worse than a dull one.

The wheelbarrows are the tools we lose most often to neglect. Keep the tires pumped, because a soft tire makes a light load feel heavy and turns the paths to ruts. Tip barrows against the shed wall after use so rain does not collect in them. A barrow left full of wet compost over a weekend is a barrow with a rusted tray by fall.

The shed is locked with a combination padlock. The combination changes at the April work day and is given to plot holders and row captains in person, never by text or email. If you are the last one out, check that both doors are latched and the lock is snapped shut, not just hung on the hasp.

Volunteer Scheduling

Shared work days are the first and third Saturday of each month from April through November, starting early and ending at noon. That schedule is fixed so that the neighbors know when to expect trucks, tools, and voices, and so that nobody has to guess. If a work day has to move for weather, the change goes on the gate board and out on the email list by Thursday evening, and the rain date is the following Saturday.

Every plot holder gives 18 hours of shared work each season, tracked on the sign-in sheet at the shed. Shared work means work that helps the whole lot: paths, compost, the shed, the fence, the water line, the front border, and the school and neighbor programs. Time spent on your own plot does not count. Members who cannot do the physical work can meet the same hours by handling sign-in, keeping the log, cooking for the fall dinner, or running the plant sale table. Hours are not carried over from one season to the next, and a member who ends a season far short of the hours is not offered a plot the next year.

Last spring the roster had 58 names on it, counting plot holders, waiting-list volunteers, and the handful of neighbors who come for the company. That is more than we can use on a single work day, so the Program Coordinator posts a task list on the gate board on the Wednesday before each work day, and members sign up for a task rather than just showing up. A work day with a task list gets twice the work done in the same morning. Show up without signing up and you will be handed a rake, which is fine, but do not expect the job you wanted.

Each row has a row captain, chosen from the plot holders on that row, who watches the row between work days, welcomes new gardeners, and speaks for the row at the fall meeting. Row captains are not police. They ask, they help, and they report to the Program Coordinator when asking and helping have not worked. A row captain who wants a break can hand the role to another plot holder on the row for the rest of the season with a note to the office.

Plot Assignment Rules

A full plot on the old lot is about 200 square feet, and a half plot is about 100 square feet. The old lot has 38 in-ground plots and 22 raised beds. The raised beds are reserved first for members who have trouble kneeling and second for the school program, and only then offered to the general list. The fee for the season is $48 for a full plot and $28 for a half plot, due with the plot agreement and not refundable after the first work day. Members who cannot pay the fee should talk to the Treasurer; nobody has ever been turned away over the fee, and nobody needs to explain why they are asking.

Renewals are due by March 31. A plot not renewed by then goes to the waiting list, no exceptions, because the list is long and the people on it have been patient. As of this spring the waiting list has 37 names. The list is kept in the order people asked, with two exceptions: a member who lost a plot to lead testing goes to the top, and a household that already holds a plot goes to the bottom for a second one. No household holds more than two plots.

A plot must be planted by mid-June and kept up through the season. Kept up means weeded, watered, and harvested; it does not mean tidy, and it does not mean pretty. A plot that goes untended for a month gets a note from the row captain, then a note from the office, and then, if nothing changes, it is turned over to the next name on the list. A member who knows they will be away for a stretch can arrange with the row captain for a neighbor to water and pick, and a plot with an arrangement on file is never turned over.

Plots are numbered from the gate outward and marked with a stake at the path corner. Do not move stakes, and do not extend a plot into the path. Paths are shared and need to stay wide enough for a barrow. Anything growing across the path line on a work day gets trimmed by whoever is trimming paths, without a note.

Composting

The garden composts in three bins along the back fence, each about a yard square and built from pallets. Bin one is for fresh material, bin two is cooking, and bin three is finished or nearly so. Only the tool captain and the compost captain move material between bins, so that a bin does not get restarted the week before it was ready.

Greens and browns go in at roughly one part green to three parts brown by volume. Greens are plant trimmings, spent crops, coffee grounds from Miller's Bakery, and grass with no weed seed. Browns are dry leaves, straw, shredded cardboard, and the stalks from last season. No meat, no dairy, no cooked food, no pet waste, no diseased plants, no weeds that have gone to seed, and no bindweed at all, even the roots. A bin that is turned every second week and kept as damp as a wrung-out sponge will heat to about 140 degrees in the middle, and that heat is what kills the seeds and the pests we do not want back.

Finished compost is screened through the half-inch sifter that hangs on the bin and used first on the raised beds and the front border, then offered to plots by the wheelbarrow. Members do not take compost home. The standard fill mix for a new raised bed on the old lot has been 40% compost and the rest screened topsoil, and we top up the beds each spring because the mix settles.

Members with plot waste that does not belong in the bins, mainly diseased tomato plants and seedy weeds, put it in the marked can by the shed, and it goes out with the city green waste. If the can is full, bag it and leave it beside the can, not in the compost.

Seasonal Calendar

The season opens with the first work day of April, when the water is turned on, the tools are sharpened, the shed combination changes, and the plot stakes are checked against the assignment list. Members who have not signed a plot agreement by the opening work day do not have a plot yet, no matter what they were told in the winter.

April and May are for building. Paths are edged, raised beds are topped up, the front border is planted, and the compost bins are emptied of finished material. Cool crops go in as soon as the ground can be worked. The last frost usually lands in the third week of April, and warm crops wait until after it. The plant sale is the last Saturday of May, and it is the garden's largest single source of money outside plot fees.

June through August are for growing and for watering. Work days shift to path care, mulching, and the water line. The school program visits run from the second week of June until the school closes for the summer, and again in September. The Program Coordinator posts the visit calendar on the gate board so members know which mornings the raised beds by the gate will be busy.

September and October are for harvest and for the fall planting of garlic and cover crops. The fall meeting is the second Saturday of October, in the community center, and every plot holder is expected. The fall dinner follows at the end of October. Tickets are $23 and cover the meal for one; members who worked the kitchen eat free.

November is for closing. Beds are cleared, cover crop is checked, hoses are drained and coiled, the water is shut off at the meter, tools are oiled, and the bins are given a final turn. The last work day is the Saturday before Thanksgiving. From then until April, the lot is closed except for the monthly walk-through by the Program Coordinator and the winter seed swap, which Miller's Bakery hosts on the last Saturday of February.

Safety

The first-aid kit hangs inside the shed door and is checked and restocked every 60 days by the tool captain, with the date of the last check written on the tape on the lid. If you use anything from the kit, write it on the tape so it gets replaced. For anything beyond a scrape or a splinter, call emergency services first and the office second. The address of the lot and the nearest cross street are on the gate board and on the inside of the shed door.

Heat is the risk we see most. When the heat index is above 95 degrees, work days end early or move to the following weekend, and no shared work is scheduled after midmorning. Drink before you are thirsty, work in the shade when there is any, and watch the person beside you, because the one who is overheating is usually the last to notice.

Lift nothing over 55 pounds alone. That is a full bag of fill or a full barrow of wet compost, and both are two-person jobs or many-trip jobs. Do not stand on the top two rungs of a ladder. Do not run the branch shredder without gloves and glasses, and never with a child within reach. Tetanus shots are the member's own business, but the office recommends being current.

Children are welcome on the lot with the adult who brought them, and that adult is responsible for them. School groups follow the youth group rules on the gate board: the visiting group brings its own adults, and garden volunteers are hosts, not supervisors. Young children stay out of the shed, off the compost bins, and away from the row spigots, which are not toys no matter how hot it is.

Dogs stay on a leash and off the beds. Smoking is not allowed on the lot. Glass of any kind stays out of the garden, because a broken jar in a bed is a hazard for years.
